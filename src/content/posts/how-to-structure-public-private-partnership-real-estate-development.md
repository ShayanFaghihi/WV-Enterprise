Most public-private partnership real estate development deals don't fail at the negotiating table. They fail months earlier — when a site hasn't been properly evaluated, when the public benefit hasn't been defined, or when a financial model gets handed to a developer who can't make the numbers work. By the time the deal collapses, everyone has spent time and political capital they won't get back.

Getting to close requires a structured process, not optimism. The team at WV Development has worked through that process across more than 20 major projects — from a $1B mixed-use redevelopment in Pittsburgh to a $2.5M EDA-funded aerotropolis initiative in Atlanta — and the pattern that separates successful transactions from stalled ones is consistent.

This guide walks through each stage in sequence. By the end, you'll know how to assess site readiness, build a model a developer will take seriously, run a procurement process that attracts qualified partners, and move a deal from term sheet to groundbreaking.

## Start With Site Readiness: What Makes a Parcel P3-Viable

Before you approach a private partner—or issue a single RFQ—you need to know exactly what you're bringing to the table. A public-private partnership real estate development deal moves faster, and closes more cleanly, when the public side has done its homework on the site first.

Start with ownership and title. Confirm who holds the parcel, identify any liens, reversionary clauses, deed restrictions, or competing claims, and resolve ambiguities before they surface in due diligence. Environmental conditions follow immediately: Phase I assessments at minimum, Phase II where the site history warrants it. A developer who discovers contamination after submitting a proposal doesn't stay at the table.

Zoning and entitlements come next—and they're not just a checklist item. Map what the parcel is permitted to do today against what your deal structure actually requires. Rezonings and variances add time and political risk; know that cost before you promise a developer a timeline. Pair this with an honest infrastructure capacity review: water, sewer, power, and transportation access. Gaps here become negotiating points later, or deal-killers if you ignore them now.

### Score the Site Before You Market It

Run each candidate parcel against three criteria before outreach begins:

* **Mission fit:** Does the project type align with the agency's economic or community development mandate?
* **Community benefit:** What does the surrounding area need—housing, jobs, services—and can this site deliver it?
* **Financial feasibility:** Is there a realistic capital stack given land value, development costs, and projected returns?

This site evaluation and land use strategy work is unglamorous, but it is the foundation every subsequent step depends on.

## Run a Market Feasibility Study Before You Recruit a Partner

Recruiting a private partner before you understand what the market will actually support is one of the most common ways a public-private partnership real estate development process stalls. A credible feasibility study does two things simultaneously: it disciplines the public agency's expectations and it gives a prospective developer something concrete to underwrite.

Start by quantifying the demand drivers that determine whether a project pencils. Employment growth trends, population shifts, transit access, and household income thresholds all shape what product types a site can absorb and at what pace. Comparable transactions—similar developments in similar markets—give you a realistic basis for pro forma assumptions before anyone enters a negotiation.

### Frame the Market for Federal and Institutional Capital

If federal funding is part of your financing strategy, the framing of your market assessment matters as much as the data itself. The Economic Development Administration and similar institutions evaluate projects through a regional competitiveness lens—job creation, economic multiplier effects, distressed community indicators. Aerotropolis Atlanta, one of the projects in WV Development's [featured projects](https://wvdevelopmentllc.com#projects) portfolio, was structured around exactly this kind of EDA-aligned market narrative, supported by a $2.5M federal investment.

Highest and best use analysis is where public goals and private returns either align or diverge. A municipality may want affordable housing; the market may support mixed-income; a developer needs a minimum return on cost. A rigorous feasibility process surfaces that tension early, before it becomes a deal-breaking dispute in year two of negotiations.

Skipping this step doesn't save time. It moves the conflict later, where it costs more.

## Define the Public Benefit and Set Your Non-Negotiables Early

A public-private partnership real estate development deal lives or dies on one question councils and auditors will always ask: what does the public actually get? If you can't answer that clearly before the RFP drops, you're not ready to go to market.

Start by naming the community outcomes you're optimizing for—jobs created and retained, affordable or workforce housing units delivered, square footage allocated to minority- and small-business tenants, access to transit. These aren't aspirational talking points. They're the foundation of your investment rationale, and they need to be written down before you invite any private partner into the room.

Documenting that rationale serves two functions. It gives your governing body a defensible record of why public land, subsidy, or credit enhancement was committed. And it tells prospective partners exactly where you will not move.

### Translate Policy Goals Into Measurable Benchmarks

Once you've established your outcomes, convert them into performance requirements that can survive a term sheet negotiation:

* Minimum local and minority hiring thresholds, tied to construction milestones
* Affordable unit count and AMI targets, with clawback provisions if delivery slips
* Small business set-aside percentage for ground-floor retail or market space
* Reporting intervals and the agency or authority responsible for verification

WV Development builds these benchmarks into the deal structure before procurement opens—not after a preferred developer is selected—because inserting them later almost always costs the public side leverage and time.

Non-negotiables defined early become protective covenants. Defined late, they become last-minute demands that stall negotiations or kill deals.

## Choose the Right P3 Structure for Your Risk Profile

![](/images/posts/pexels-kindelmedia-7578859.jpg "Aerial view of mixed-use urban redevelopment site in early construction phase")

No single P3 transaction structure fits every project. The right model depends on what risk each party can actually manage, how long the deal needs to perform, and what your enabling legislation permits. Getting this wrong early means renegotiating later — usually at a cost to both schedule and political capital.

### Four Structures Worth Knowing

**Ground lease.** The public entity retains land ownership and leases to a private developer for a fixed term — typically 50 to 99 years. This protects public assets while giving the developer the collateral needed to finance construction. It works well when the parcel has clear title, strong market demand, and a jurisdiction that lacks authority to sell.

**Joint venture.** Public and private parties co-own the development entity, sharing upside and downside. This structure demands aligned interests from day one and careful attention to governance. It suits projects where the public sector brings land or permits and the private sector brings capital and execution.

**Concession agreement.** A private operator takes on management and capital responsibility for a defined period in exchange for revenue rights. Airport and transit-adjacent projects often use this model because it transfers operational risk to the party best equipped to price it.

**Design-build-finance.** The private partner designs, builds, and finances the asset — sometimes operating it as well. This compresses the schedule and shifts delivery risk, but it requires a capital stack sophisticated enough to attract institutional lenders.

Match the structure to your statutory authority first, then to your timeline and financing complexity. A municipality with limited bonding capacity and a 10-year political horizon has a different risk profile than a redevelopment authority assembling a mixed-use district over 25 years.

## Build the Financial Model That Gets a Developer to the Table

A developer evaluating a public-private partnership real estate development opportunity is running one number above everything else: their internal rate of return. If the project doesn't pencil at a return threshold that justifies the risk, they walk—regardless of how compelling the public mission is. Your job before issuing any solicitation is to close that gap on paper first.

Start by mapping the full cost structure: land, infrastructure, soft costs, phasing timeline, and operating assumptions. Then identify where the project breaks. Most mixed-use or community-serving developments carry a financing gap because market rents or sales prices don't support conventional debt and equity alone. That gap is where public tools do their work.

### Layer Public Financing Strategically

Stack tools in the order they address different parts of the capital structure:

* **TIF (Tax Increment Financing):** Covers infrastructure costs tied to future tax growth—useful for site prep, utilities, and public realm improvements.
* **New Markets Tax Credits (NMTC):** Reduces equity requirements in low-income census tracts by attracting CDFI and institutional capital at below-market rates.
* **HUD grants and EDA awards:** Fund predevelopment, planning, or catalytic infrastructure that de-risks early project phases. The Aerotropolis Atlanta initiative drew $2.5M in EDA funding precisely to establish that foundation.
* **Gap financing:** Subordinate public loans or deferred land value fill what the stack above doesn't cover.

Model each layer's effect on developer IRR explicitly. Then calculate the public return on subsidy—jobs created, tax base expansion, or housing units delivered per dollar committed. That two-sided model is what moves a term sheet forward.

## Run a Procurement Process That Attracts Qualified Partners

A procurement document is not a formality—it is your first filter. An RFQ or RFP that clearly states the required project structure, P3 experience thresholds, and community benefit obligations will discourage marginal respondents and give serious developers the information they need to commit internal resources to a response. Vague solicitations attract volume; specific ones attract quality.

Draft the document to answer the questions a qualified developer will ask before they spend money on a response: What is the proposed deal structure? What financing gap exists? What community requirements are non-negotiable? If your financial model is reasonably mature at this stage—as it should be—surface the key assumptions. Transparency at this point is not a negotiating concession; it signals that the public side knows what it is doing.

### Shortlisting Criteria That Matter

Evaluate respondents on three dimensions: technical capacity to deliver at the project's scale and complexity, financial strength to close and carry the deal, and demonstrated public-private partnership real estate development experience—not just private development experience. Those are different skill sets.

Structured interviews are where you learn what the paper cannot tell you. Ask respondents to walk through their financing assumptions for this specific site, not a project they have already built. A developer who cannot defend their pro forma in a room with your team will not perform better in negotiations.

Bring an owner's representative into the evaluation process. An experienced owner's rep can identify gaps in a respondent's P3 procurement experience that may not be visible to staff who live inside the deal every day.

## Align Stakeholders Before Negotiations Stall Your Deal

Most public-private partnership real estate development deals don't collapse in the negotiation room. They collapse six months earlier, when a city council member hears about the project from a constituent instead of from staff, or when an anchor institution realizes its transportation access wasn't part of the plan.

Map your stakeholders before you issue a single RFQ. That means elected officials, neighborhood associations, anchor institutions, advocacy groups, faith communities, and any agency with a regulatory or funding role. Not every stakeholder carries equal weight, but every stakeholder you ignore carries risk.

### Structure Engagement Around Deal Decision Points

General community meetings generate general feedback. That's not useful at the term sheet stage. Instead, tie your engagement sessions to specific decisions:

* **Site and program definition:** Are community priorities reflected in the development mix?
* **Partner selection criteria:** Do local hiring, minority contracting, or affordability requirements need to be written into the RFP?
* **Financial structure:** Are there public benefits—workforce housing, transit access, small business space—that require early commitment from the developer?

Document every session. Summarize input in writing, share it back with participants, and show how it shaped the deal. That paper trail doesn't just build trust—it reduces the exposure to legal challenges and political reversals that can stop execution long after close.

## Execute Through Close: From Term Sheet to Groundbreaking

Reaching a signed term sheet is significant. It is not the finish line. The deals that fall apart between agreement-in-principle and groundbreaking usually fail for the same reasons: vague milestones, unclear accountability, and stakeholders who feel surprised by decisions they thought they had already approved.

### Lock the Development Agreement to Specific Outcomes

A well-drafted development agreement does more than document the deal—it creates the operating structure for the next several years of the project. Negotiate milestone dates with consequences attached. Clawback provisions should trigger automatically if the developer misses defined benchmarks: permit submission, financing commitment, construction start. Reporting requirements belong in the agreement itself, not as a handshake expectation. Specify what gets reported, to whom, and on what cadence before both parties sign.

The agreement is also where the public benefit commitments established earlier in the process get converted into enforceable obligations. Community hiring targets, affordable unit ratios, phasing requirements—each one should have a verification mechanism, not just an aspiration.

Assign an owner's representative once the agreement executes. In public-private partnership real estate development, the public agency rarely has dedicated bandwidth to manage vendor coordination, monitor construction schedules, and track contractor performance simultaneously. An owner's rep fills that gap. Their job is schedule integrity and early problem identification—surfacing issues when they are still solvable.

Capital project assistance through close means maintaining a live dashboard: financing milestones, permit status, draw schedules, and construction completion against baseline. Construction implementation is where commitments made at the negotiating table either hold or quietly erode. Measure both.

## Frequently asked questions

### How does a public-private partnership work for real estate development?

A public agency contributes land, entitlements, or financing tools while a private developer brings capital, construction capacity, and market execution. Responsibilities, revenues, and risks are split by agreement. The structure only works when both parties have clear objectives, aligned timelines, and a shared understanding of what the market will support.

### What is the difference between a ground lease and a joint venture P3 structure?

In a ground lease, the public agency retains land ownership and collects rent while the developer builds and operates. In a joint venture, both parties hold equity and share upside and risk. Ground leases are simpler to close; joint ventures require deeper alignment on governance, exit rights, and profit distribution.

### How do cities fund the public side of a P3 infrastructure project?

Common tools include federal grants such as EDA economic development awards, tax increment financing, HUD programs, and proceeds from asset disposition. Agencies may also use bond authority or in-kind contributions like land and infrastructure improvements. The right mix depends on project type, community objectives, and which funding sources the project can competitively qualify for.

### What qualifications should a government agency look for in a P3 developer?

Look for demonstrated experience closing comparable transactions, not just managing construction. A strong P3 developer understands entitlements, financing structures, and stakeholder dynamics. Relevant track record, capital relationships, and the ability to navigate public process without stalling momentum are more predictive of success than firm size or marketing materials.
